Two-sided: the value of concealment

Every page so far uses a one-sided model: the Schemer sees the Monitor's exact state and action, and only the Monitor is uncertain (about the Schemer's type). A genuinely two-sided model -- solved with a sibling HSVI solver where each player holds a belief over the other's hidden state -- lets the Schemer's view of the Monitor be imperfect too. This page walks through the actual selling point that extension buys: a defender can gain real, measurable value from a real attacker's not knowing exactly what it did, something a one-sided model cannot even express, since its attacker already sees everything. A small 2-round toy is used throughout, small enough to check against an exact, independently-computed LP ground truth at every step.

First attempt: nothing happened

The obvious experiment: take the validated 2-round toy, build a second copy where the Schemer's observation of the Monitor's action is made perfectly accurate instead of noisy, and compare. Result: identical to three decimals -- same value, same solved policy, same best-response sequence. Hiding the Monitor's action bought nothing.
The reason traces back to this toy's own solved strategy: the Monitor's optimal policy is pure -- Audit at round 0, unconditionally, every time. A pure strategy is perfectly predictable from the game's structure alone; there is nothing left for an observation to reveal that the Schemer couldn't already deduce for free. Concealment only has value when what's being concealed is genuinely randomized.

Why this toy never mixes

Reducing round 0 to its own 2x2 subgame (ignoring round 1, Monitor: Pass/Audit vs. Schemer: Honest/Attack) exposes the problem directly. Monitor's expected payoff, weighted by the 25% prior that the Schemer is real:
Schemer: HonestSchemer: Attack
Monitor: Pass0.0−2.5
Monitor: Audit−1.0−1.0
Catching an attacker under Audit costs the Monitor exactly the same as auditing an honest actor (catch_prob(Audit)=1, so a caught attack is fully neutralized and just costs the flat audit fee either way) -- the bottom row is constant. The Schemer is dead indifferent between Attack and Honest whenever Audited, so the Monitor never needs to hedge against it: a pure choice is always enough. No tension, no mixing, nothing for concealment to protect.

Retuning for a genuine mixed strategy

Classic inspection games need catching to be a genuine win for the inspector, not just a break-even avoided cost. Added one constant, C_CATCH_BONUS: a reward for actually catching a real attack under Audit, on top of the flat audit fee. That makes the bottom-right cell strictly better than the bottom-left for the Monitor, which is exactly the cyclic (matching-pennies-style) structure a mixed equilibrium requires:
Schemer: HonestSchemer: Attack
Monitor: Pass0.0−2.5
Monitor: Audit−1.0+1.0
Hand-derived the round-0 indifference condition analytically: P(Pass) = C_CATCH_BONUS / (10 + C_CATCH_BONUS), valid for any C_CATCH_BONUS > 0. Swept it from 0 to 10 in the full two-round LP ground truth (not just this reduced subgame) to confirm the prediction survives the round-1 continuation -- it does. C_CATCH_BONUS=0 reproduces the old pure result exactly; every value above 0 produces a genuinely fractional Monitor strategy.
Picked C_CATCH_BONUS = 2.0 (marked above): a clean, comfortably interior mix -- Monitor plays Audit 83.3% of the time at round 0, Pass the remaining 16.7%. Only two reward entries in the whole toy change to get here; everything else -- states, transitions, observations, priors -- is untouched.

The result

With a genuinely mixed Monitor strategy in play, the same noisy-vs-full-info comparison now shows a real, verified gap -- confirmed twice, independently: once from the exact LP ground truth over the full enumerated strategy space, and once from the actual HSVI solver (bar = ground truth, marker = solver's best-response-verified value, thin line = solver's converged bracket).

The Monitor is worth about 0.05 more (in this toy's units) when the Schemer can't be sure what it did. Small in absolute terms -- it's a 2-round toy -- but the direction and rough size match exactly between two completely independent computations (LP duality on the full enumerated 8x8 strategy matrix, and HSVI + best-response verification in the actual two-sided solver), which is what makes this a real result rather than solver noise. This is the first finding in this project that a one-sided model cannot produce even in principle: its attacker already sees the defender's realized action by construction, so there is never anything to conceal.

How much noise is actually needed?

The comparison above is two points: fully deterministic vs. the toy's current noise level. Interpolating between them (O2(a1) = alpha*noisy(a1) + (1-alpha)*deterministic(a1), alpha=0 is full-info, alpha=1 is the toy as built) turns it into a curve, and the shape is more interesting than a smooth trade-off: the value rises steadily off full-info, then hits a sharp corner and goes completely flat.
The Monitor gets the entire defensive benefit of concealment from a small amount of noise -- roughly 20% of the way from deterministic toward the toy's current level -- and every bit of ambiguity beyond that threshold (alpha ≈ 0.197) is wasted, not additional protection. Once the Schemer can no longer be certain which round-0 action occurred, its best response is already forced into the same hedge it would make under much heavier noise; piling on more ambiguity past that point doesn't change its decision further. Concealment has a threshold of "enough," not a "more is always better" relationship.
Built entirely from the same LP ground-truth machinery already confirmed against the actual solver at both endpoints (alpha=0 and alpha=1, marked below) -- the interior of the curve itself hasn't been separately re-run through the solver, since it's generated by the identical enumeration/LP code that matched the solver at those two points to two decimals. Worth a spot-check at an interior alpha if this threshold shape ends up load- bearing for anything beyond illustrating the methodology.
The point above was made cleanly and cheaply at toy scale, with an exact ground truth to check against. Tried it at full scale (the real 8-round, 8-problem game) anyway.

Confirmed at full scale -- with a caveat

First, under the full game's own original reward structure (no bonus): an exact repeat of the toy's original null result. Full-info and noisy brackets, and even the best-response sequences (flat at exactly -57.764 across 7 depths, in both games) matched precisely. A likely reason ties back to an already-documented finding elsewhere in this project: the worst-case Schemer's actual policy attacks almost everywhere, almost always, regardless of belief -- if its behavior barely responds to information it already has, there's nothing left for concealment to change.
Applying the same C_CATCH_BONUS fix (scaled to the same bonus/catastrophe ratio the toy used) brought the effect back, same direction: best-response -52.279 (noisy) vs. -52.308 (full-info), a real gap of about 0.03 in the Monitor's favor under concealment. But relative to the game's own scale, that's roughly 30x smaller proportionally than the toy's effect (0.06% of the full game's value vs. 1.7% of the toy's) -- the mechanism's contribution appears to get diluted across 8 rounds' worth of other reward-driving structure it doesn't touch. The finding generalizes qualitatively (concealing a genuinely mixed strategy has real value, confirmed at both scales) but not proportionally -- a genuine, honest result rather than either a clean confirmation or a clean failure. Not chased further (tighter eps, isolating which specific rounds carry the effect), consistent with treating this as a proof of concept for the methodology.
