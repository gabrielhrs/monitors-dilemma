Fixed schedule vs. two real belief-adaptive trials

The top row is the best possible fixed (non-adaptive, committed-in-advance) audit schedule from naive_baseline.py. The two rows below are two actual trials from the real solved game (games/convergence_tree.json) -- same 8 rounds, same rules, but the Monitor's action reacts to its belief as it updates. Rounds boxed in yellow are where the adaptive policy's action differs from what the fixed schedule commits to regardless. Dashed boxes are extrapolated past the 6 iterations actually run (reasoned from the same catch-probability logic established elsewhere in this project), not directly observed in the trace.

Confirmed Schemer early: R1's audit reveals a real attack, so the Monitor keeps its second audit for R2 rather than the fixed schedule's habit of just repeating the same plan -- here that happens to match anyway. The real divergence shows up at R4, R5 and R8: with belief now at 1.0 and no audit budget left, Cheap gives literally 0% protection on these unbounded-domain rounds (measured directly from the elicitation data), so paying its -0.2 cost buys nothing -- the adaptive policy passes for free instead of wasting it, something a schedule fixed in advance has no way to know it's safe to do.
Reassured early, flagged late: R1's audit comes back honest, and belief drops from 0.25 to 0.044 -- but not to exactly zero (the solver's own worst-case adversary strategy allows a Schemer to sometimes stay honest, see the write-up). Because of that, the Monitor still keeps cheap-checking bounded-domain rounds rather than assuming total safety. That vigilance pays off at R7: belief enters that round at just 0.030, a Cheap-check there flags a real attack, and belief jumps to 1.0 entering R8 -- reversing six rounds of accumulated reassurance in a single observation. A fixed schedule, having already spent both its audits on R1 and R2, has no budget and no mechanism left to react to this at all.
