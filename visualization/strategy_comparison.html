Fixed schedule vs. two real belief-adaptive trials

The top row is the best possible fixed (non-adaptive, committed-in-advance) audit schedule from scripts/naive_baseline_v2.py. The two rows below are two actual trials from the real solved game -- same 8 rounds, same rules, but the Monitor's action reacts to its belief as it updates. Rounds boxed in yellow are where the adaptive policy's action differs from what the fixed schedule commits to regardless. Dashed boxes are extrapolated past the 5 rounds each trial actually reached before its trace ends, using the fully-solved belief-to-action policy at that exact (round, budget, belief) point (see Reconstructed policy) rather than general reasoning -- not directly observed in this specific trial's trace, but read off the real solved policy all the same.

Confirmed Schemer early: R1's audit reveals a real attack, so the Monitor keeps its second audit for R2 rather than the fixed schedule's habit of just repeating the same plan -- here that happens to match anyway. Both audits are now spent and belief is pinned at 1.0, but the remaining rounds aren't all treated alike: R4 (reverse_string) and R5 (count_vowels) are unbounded, so Cheap's catch odds are exactly 0% there and the adaptive policy passes for free instead of paying -0.2 for nothing. R6 (is_prime) is bounded with real 10.9% catch odds, so the policy still spends the Cheap check there. R7 (sum_of_digits) is technically "bounded" too, but its reachable-domain catch odds are only 0.025% -- close enough to zero that the policy treats it exactly like an unbounded round and passes. R8 (remove_duplicates) is unbounded and passes as expected. That's the real divergence from the fixed schedule (which just keeps paying for Cheap regardless): a schedule fixed in advance has no way to know, per problem, which of these checks is actually worth its cost.
Reassured early, confirmed late: R1 is a free Pass; R2's audit comes back honest, and belief drops from 0.25 to 0.041 -- but not to exactly zero (the solver's own worst-case adversary strategy allows a Schemer to sometimes stay honest, see the write-up). Rather than spend its second audit right away, the adaptive policy holds it back: R3 is a Cheap check on fibonacci (16.7% catch odds) that comes back clear, nudging belief down further to 0.035. That held-back audit pays off at R4: it reveals a real attack, and belief jumps straight from 0.035 to 1.0 entering R5 -- reversing three rounds of accumulated reassurance in one hard signal. A fixed schedule, having already spent both its audits on R1 and R2, has no budget and no mechanism left to react to this at all. Worth noting: a Cheap check essentially never comes back FLAG in this game -- catch odds are small enough (see the note on strategy above) that the solver's own exploration rarely finds that branch worth walking. Audit, not a lucky Cheap flag, is what supplies decisive signal here (see the worked trial for the same point in more detail).
